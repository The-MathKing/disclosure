## Branch A: The Interaction Replicates (As Hypothesized)

*Use this branch if both the main effects and the interaction effect (Product Typology × AI Disclosure) are significant.*

**\subsection{Theoretical Contributions}**
This study demonstrates a robust authenticity penalty for AI disclosures. Our data yielded the hypothesized interaction with product typology and the moderated mediation pathway, indicating that this mediation was only significant for fully AI-generated content. This contributes to source credibility theory by identifying product typology as a critical boundary condition, and suggests a non-linear threshold where consumers may tolerate minor AI assistance but strictly penalize full AI generation in experience goods.

**\subsection{Managerial Implications}**
For marketers navigating emerging FTC guidelines, these results provide a nuanced takeaway: disclosing full AI generation heavily penalizes perceived authenticity and trust, particularly for experience goods. However, because the penalty for AI assistance is significantly weaker (or non-significant), marketers may be able to leverage AI for drafting assistance without incurring a catastrophic loss of consumer trust, provided human oversight is maintained. Marketers of experiential products should proceed with extreme caution before replacing human reviews with fully AI-generated text.

## Branch B: Only Main Effects Replicate (No Interaction)

*Use this branch if AI Disclosure significantly lowers authenticity, but Product Typology fails to moderate this effect (i.e., the penalty is universally applied across both search and experience goods).*

**\subsection{Theoretical Contributions}**
This study demonstrates a robust and universal authenticity penalty for AI disclosures. Contrary to our hypothesis, product typology did not moderate this effect: consumers penalized AI-generated reviews equally across both search and experience goods. This contributes to source credibility theory by suggesting that the mere presence of AI automation fundamentally violates consumer expectations of human authorship, regardless of whether the product evaluation relies on objective facts or subjective sensory experience. The findings indicate that the authenticity penalty is a generalized heuristic rather than a context-dependent evaluation.

**\subsection{Managerial Implications}**
For marketers navigating emerging FTC guidelines, these results offer a stark warning: disclosing AI generation heavily penalizes perceived authenticity and trust, and this penalty applies universally across product categories. Marketers cannot rely on the objective, fact-based nature of search goods to shield them from the AI authenticity penalty. Consequently, brands should proceed with extreme caution before replacing human reviews with fully AI-generated text in any product category, as the loss of consumer trust appears to be broad and robust.
